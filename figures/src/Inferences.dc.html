What other repeat diseases suggest about the Huntington's disease threshold

Inferences drawn from myotonic dystrophy, fragile X and facioscapulohumeral dystrophy. None has yet been tested in Huntington's disease.

1. A sharp threshold can come from a repeat binding up a limited supply of a regulator

In myotonic dystrophy the CUG repeat RNA binds MBNL1 until the cell's supply runs short, and raising MBNL1 rescued model mice. Expanded polyglutamine binds the co-activator CBP, and raising CBP rescued cultured neurons. For Huntington's disease, raising the bound regulator should move the threshold above 150 CAGs.

2. Losing a regulator can return a cell to an earlier developmental program

Without MBNL1, adult muscle keeps fetal splice forms. In phase D, Huntington's disease neurons switch on embryonic patterning genes. PRC2, which keeps those genes silent, is the obvious candidate regulator; the direct evidence so far shows mutant huntingtin stimulating PRC2 rather than inhibiting it.

3. One repeat can have two thresholds, each with its own mechanism

The FMR1 repeat produces extra toxic RNA at 55–200 and silences its gene above 200. Huntington's disease neurons lose identity genes gradually from 150 CAGs, while the derepression crisis becomes common only above about 350 and does not grow with length once begun, which fits a second mechanism with its own threshold.

4. Huntington's disease differs from the silencing diseases

Long FMR1 repeats raise or silence their own gene's RNA. The rise runs with repeat length across the premutation range and reaches ten times normal at the long end. HTT RNA showed no detectable change past 150 CAGs, so silencing of HTT itself does not explain the threshold. The sequencing used could not see how HTT RNA is processed, which points to measuring the short HTT1a fragment directly.

5. One disease is caused by the repeat shortening

Shortening the D4Z4 array below about 11 units strips the methylation that silences it and switches on DUX4, a gene used only in the early embryo. The endpoint matches the derepression crisis in Huntington's neurons, where developmental transcription factors come on in a cell that should have them shut, but it is reached by the repeat getting shorter rather than longer. A short array causes disease only on a chromosome with the 4qA variant, and a second form arises at normal length when SMCHD1, which maintains the methylation, is mutated: the silencing is what matters, and length by itself does not.

Sources: Lin et al., Hum Mol Genet 2006; Kanadia et al., PNAS 2006; Nucifora et al., Science 2001; Seong et al., Hum Mol Genet 2010; Tassone et al., Am J Hum Genet 2000; Depienne and Mandel, Am J Hum Genet 2021; Handsaker et al., Cell 2025; Kashin et al., Cell 2026; Cascella et al., Front Neurol 2018.
