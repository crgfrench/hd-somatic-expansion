Repeat expansion diseases: normal and disease-causing repeat lengths

Lengths a person is born with, in repeat units, on a logarithmic scale. For Huntington's disease the disease-causing range starts at 36, a threshold known since the gene was found in 1993. Forty or more always leads to the disease; 36 to 39 carries a reduced risk that rises with age, and some carriers never develop it. The dashed line at 150 is a different measurement, made on a single neuron decades later: by then the repeat in that cell has grown well beyond what the person inherited, and cells in one brain differ widely. Above about 150 in a given cell, that cell begins to change. Only Huntington's disease has been measured this way.

A longer repeat at birth puts more neurons past 150 within a lifetime

The repeat a person inherits is the same in every cell at birth. From then on each cell’s copy grows on its own, slowly at first and then faster once it passes about 90. A longer inherited length shortens the slow part, so more of that person’s neurons reach 150 during a lifetime, and symptoms start earlier.

What other repeat diseases suggest about the Huntington's disease threshold

Inferences drawn from myotonic dystrophy, fragile X and facioscapulohumeral dystrophy. None has yet been tested in Huntington's disease.

1. A sharp threshold can come from a repeat binding up a limited supply of a regulator

In myotonic dystrophy the CUG repeat RNA binds MBNL1 until the cell's supply runs short, and raising MBNL1 rescued model mice. Expanded polyglutamine binds the co-activator CBP, and raising CBP rescued cultured neurons. For Huntington's disease, raising the bound regulator should move the threshold above 150 CAGs.

2. Losing a regulator can return a cell to an earlier developmental program

Without MBNL1, adult muscle keeps fetal splice forms. In phase D, Huntington's disease neurons switch on embryonic patterning genes. PRC2, which keeps those genes silent, is the obvious candidate regulator; the direct evidence so far shows mutant huntingtin stimulating PRC2 rather than inhibiting it.

3. One repeat can have two thresholds, each with its own mechanism

The FMR1 repeat produces extra toxic RNA at 55–200 and silences its gene above 200. Huntington's disease neurons lose identity genes gradually from 150 CAGs, while the derepression crisis becomes common only above about 350 and does not grow with length once begun, which fits a second mechanism with its own threshold.

4. Huntington's disease differs from the silencing diseases

Long FMR1 repeats raise or silence their own gene's RNA. The rise runs with repeat length across the premutation range and reaches ten times normal at the long end. HTT RNA showed no detectable change past 150 CAGs, so silencing of HTT itself does not explain the threshold. The sequencing used could not see how HTT RNA is processed, which points to measuring the short HTT1a fragment directly.

5. One disease is caused by the repeat shortening

Shortening the D4Z4 array below about 11 units strips the methylation that silences it and switches on DUX4, a gene used only in the early embryo. The endpoint matches the derepression crisis in Huntington's neurons, where developmental transcription factors come on in a cell that should have them shut, but it is reached by the repeat getting shorter rather than longer. A short array causes disease only on a chromosome carrying the 4qA variant, and a second form arises at normal length when SMCHD1, which maintains the methylation, is mutated: the silencing is what matters, and length by itself does not.

1

How the repeat does damage sets the cutoff

Repeats in the coding sequence act through the protein and cause disease at 12–55 units. Repeats elsewhere act through toxic RNA, gene silencing or repeat proteins and need 30–680. CTG causes disease at 50 in DMPK and 74 in ATXN8OS; CAG causes Huntington's disease at 36 inside the coding sequence.

2

Gene-silencing diseases sit near 200

Fragile X (above 200), fragile XE (200) and Baratela-Scott syndrome (120) are CGG or CCG repeats in the region that controls the gene. The expanded repeat becomes methylated and the gene shuts down, which fits one mechanism with its own length requirement.

3

The glutamine cluster has two explanations

Ten CAG diseases in ten genes start at 32–55. Polyglutamine begins to aggregate near that length; the 2025 Cell paper reads 36–40 in Huntington's disease as the length at which the DNA repeat becomes unstable enough to reach 150 in a lifetime. Both depend on CAG length, so the histogram does not separate them.

4

Proteins respond to a few extra units

Alanine tracts cause disease with 4–10 extra residues (RUNX2 17 to 27, PABPN1 6–10 to 12); their repeats are stable and the diseases mostly congenital. Huntington's disease neurons show no detectable change from 36 to 150 CAGs, which fits toxicity arising at long lengths by another route.

5

SCA6 works without somatic expansion

SCA6 causes disease at 20–33 CAGs, and its repeat shows no mosaicism across brain regions or in sperm; a review rates its instability very low or absent, against high for SCA1, 2, 3 and 7. The glutamine diseases therefore include at least one that does not depend on expansion to a high threshold.

6

The long end reflects discovery history

The split between 30–120 and 200+ outside the coding sequence may shift as more repeats are found. Very long repeats were hard to detect before long-read sequencing, and most cutoffs of 200–680 belong to intronic repeats found with it since 2017.

Sources: Depienne and Mandel, Am J Hum Genet 2021 (lengths); Handsaker et al., Cell 2025; Mantuano et al., Cytogenet Genome Res 2003, citing Ishikawa et al. 1999 (brain) and Shizuka et al. 1998 (sperm) for SCA6 stability. Inferences 1–4 and 6 are drawn from the histogram and have not been tested.
